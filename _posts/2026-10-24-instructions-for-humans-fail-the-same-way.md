The laziest-reader piece on this blog was about rules written for agents. Its uncomfortable implication, in my experience, is that the reader it describes is usually not an agent at all. At my desk, the lazy reader is a person in a hurry: someone reading a handoff note between two other tasks, a checklist on a phone with one hand busy, a set of instructions opened at the moment of doing, not in a quiet moment of study. That reader is not careless. That reader is busy, and busy readers take the cheapest reading that still counts as following the instruction.

The failures I see land in the same places. An instruction to check something regularly has no trigger a hurried reader can act on; regularly becomes whenever it is remembered, which is to say after it mattered. An instruction to call someone if things look bad has no threshold; bad is a judgment the writer made privately and never handed over, so the reader has to invent it at the worst moment. An instruction to handle the old items first has no list; old is a word hiding an enumeration nobody wrote down. The research version of this failure says the same. Berry and Kamsties, studying ambiguous requirements, found that classic trigger words accounted for only a small share of the ambiguity cases; most ambiguity was missing information, a slot nobody filled. At my desk, instructions for people fail the same way: not on their wording, on their empty slots.

The repairs are the same three moves, and none of them require machinery. First, attach the intent in one line, so a reader who has to improvise at least improvises toward the purpose. Second, fill the slots with named things: a date or an event as the trigger, a number as the threshold, the actual list instead of the adjective. Third, test the result against a hurried reading before it ships: what is the cheapest compliant way to do this, and is that way acceptable. At my desk I treat it this way: if the cheapest reading is wrong, the instruction is wrong, however careful it sounds.

There is a kindness point buried in this, and at my desk it is the part worth keeping. Writing an instruction that survives a hurried reader is not a lack of trust in that reader. It is an admission about when instructions are actually read: late, fast, and mid-task. An instruction written for the reader's best moment will, at my desk, be applied in their worst one. I write it for the worst one, and both readers get it right.
