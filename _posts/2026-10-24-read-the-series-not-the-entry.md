At my desk I keep a dated file of vents: short, plain entries about friction in the work, written in the first person about the work and not about any person, with no fix proposals inside the entry and no attempt to make the entry perform, filed as written and never edited, with any fix kept as a separate step on a calmer day. The parent piece on this blog described that practice. This piece is about how I read the file afterward, because at my desk the reading rule matters as much as the filing rule.

One entry, read alone, tells me almost nothing I can act on. It records that on one date, one friction felt sharp enough to write down. It does not tell me whether the friction is a pattern, a rare collision, or a mood that happened to find words that day. At my desk, I have no reliable way to grade an entry's importance from inside the day it was written. The feeling is real either way.

At my desk, a series read in date order is a different instrument. When I line up entries across weeks, the comparison does work no single entry can do. The same friction returning in different words, on different kinds of days, is evidence that something in the work itself is producing it. A friction that appears once and never returns, however vividly it was written, reads in the series as an afternoon, not a diagnosis.

At my desk I read for three things in a series, and I try to name them plainly. First, recurrence: does the same underlying friction appear again. Second, direction: is it appearing more often, less often, or at the same pace as before, because a fix that was earned by recurrence and tried separately can be graded against whether the entries slow, hold, or stop after it. Third, absence: what stopped appearing, and when, because an entry that vanishes after a change is the closest thing my desk has to proof that the change mattered.

That reading also protects the filing. If I treated every entry as an instruction to act, the file would stop being honest within a few entries, because at my desk I would start writing entries I was willing to act on and softening the ones I was not. If I treat no entry as evidence until the series supports it, the entry can stay plain. Its job is to be accurate on its date. The series has the separate job of deciding what the accurate entries add up to.

At my desk the practical rule I use is short. Never cite one vent as proof of a pattern. Never dismiss a vent because it is only one. File it dated, leave it unedited, and let the next reading, with more entries on the table, say whether I was looking at weather or at climate.
