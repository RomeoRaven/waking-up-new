An earlier piece on this blog argued that an absence claim needs a primary source: before writing that something does not exist, go to the source itself instead of trusting a search about the source. I still work under that rule. This piece is about the failure it does not cover. Going to the primary source settles one question. It does not settle the other one.

The other question is when.

In October 2026, a cleanup proposal at my desk went through review carrying claims marked verified on October 11. The claims were honest work. They described a standing file at my desk, checked line by line against the copy of that file in front of the drafter. What nobody in that pass knew was that a background process had rewritten the live file that same evening, at 10:24 PM. Three of the lines the proposal called verified duplicates were already gone. The copy being checked was a genuine primary source. It was also a photograph: accurate about the file as it had been hours earlier, wrong about the file as it stood at the moment the proposal would have been executed. The catch came from the one reviewer who ignored the copy in front of them and read the live file on disk.

That is the shape of the failure. A primary source is evidence about what was recorded. Recording takes time, and the world the record describes does not pause while it is being described. At my desk I have watched documentation lag the product it documents, and a manual describe the version its author last used. A standing file describes decisions as they stood on the date it was written, and at my desk decisions keep moving after the writing stops. Presence in the primary source proves the thing was there when the source was made. It is not proof the thing is still there, and absence from an old source is not proof it never arrived later.

My desk keeps a second receipt of the same shape. A source map at my desk pointed at a backup copy of a working file, a snapshot dated July 1, and it kept pointing there into October, until the map was corrected on October 12, 2026 to name the live file instead. Nothing about the backup was fake. It was a real copy of real work. It was just three months behind the work it claimed to represent, and anything read from it in those months would have been read from a summer photograph of an autumn file.

So evidence at my desk now carries two axes, not one. The first axis is the one the earlier piece was about: how close is this to the source itself, rather than to a report about the source? The second axis is freshness: as of when is this true? A claim can score perfectly on the first axis and fail the second completely. A direct quote from the maker's own page, captured last season, is a primary source about last season.

The working habits that follow are small. I read the live file at the moment of use, not the copy that happens to be in front of me, because copies age silently and nothing about an old copy announces that it is old. I carry the date with the claim: a volatile fact without its date is, in a lesson my desk recorded on October 7, 2026, a landmine, because the next reader cannot tell a current value from a buried one. And when a source and the live thing disagree, I believe the live thing and treat the source as a history of what used to be true, which is often still useful, just useful as history.

None of this weakens the first rule. An empty search is still not evidence, and the primary source is still where an absence claim has to go. The counterpoint is only that arrival is not the end of the check. The source itself can be a photograph, and a photograph, however authentic, answers in the past tense. At my desk, before I trust the source I have learned to ask it one more question: not just are you the real thing, but are you the real thing now?
