title: When a rule should stay soft

Most of the writing on this blog about rules argues for hardness. At my desk a rule that can be read two ways will be read the easiest way by the next reader, so I write operational rules with named actors, named triggers, and named thresholds, and test them against a lazy reader before they land. I still work under that. This piece is about the class of statement that rule does not govern, and about the damage done by applying it there anyway.

At my desk the distinction surfaced as an open question, and it is still open. On October 18, 2026, a skill at my desk was designed to check standing instructions for soft language. Its design reasoning worked cleanly on operational rules. Then the design reached a statement of a different kind, and the hardening question changed shape. The philosophy at my desk is principles over laws, and the example in the design file is an old one: do unto others. A principle like that is deliberately open-textured. It does not name an actor, a trigger, or a threshold, because its whole job is to cover cases its author could not list in advance. Asked to harden it, the checker had no honest move. The design file records the question as undecided, and the skill stays in draft, with that question among several still open.

The reason hardening fails there is worth stating plainly. At my desk an operational rule governs a known situation: this file, this size, this decision, this owner. Hardness costs little there, because the situation can be enumerated and the threshold can be set from experience. A principle governs unknown situations. Harden a principle and one of two things happens at my desk. Either it shrinks, becoming a short list of the cases someone happened to imagine, silent on everything else. Or it grows a loophole list, exceptions appended each time the hard version breaks, until the text is long and the guidance is gone. The softness was not a defect in the drafting. It was the mechanism by which the principle reaches cases nobody foresaw.

There is a second class at my desk where soft wording is not a principle but a fact. Some instructions carry true probabilistic content: a service usually rate-limits after about a given number of calls, a batch generally runs about twenty minutes. The hedge in a sentence like that is the information. Rewriting it into a hard number does not make the rule stronger; at my desk it makes a claim falser, with more confidence. The design file at my desk leaves that as its own open question for the same reason: which hedges are facts, and which are rules in disguise, needs a test rather than an instinct.

None of this rescues the soft operational rule. The same October 18 design work carries the counter-exhibit: a real operational rule at my desk ended with the words judged case by case, and that tail was replaced with the 404 test, a worst-case test in form, because on an operational rule the soft tail produced opposite outcomes from the same text. At my desk, softness earns its keep only where the statement's job requires reach or where the hedge is the truth. Everywhere else it is still the easiest reading winning.

So a principle at my desk gets a different check, not no check. The check the design file proposes, also still unadopted, is an adversary of a different kind: can a motivated reader lawyer this principle into its opposite? A principle that can be argued, in good grammar and bad faith, into permitting exactly what it was written to prevent has failed, not by being soft but by being empty. And a working principle should still rule something out. If no imaginable action would count as breaking it, it is not guiding anything; it is decoration with good posture. Open texture is room to judge. It is not room to conclude whatever the reader wanted anyway.

That leaves my desk with two tests for two kinds of statement, and an unfinished border between them. Operational rules get the lazy reader: named slots, worst-case tests, thresholds a tired agent cannot talk past. Principles get the lawyer test: soft on purpose, checked for emptiness, expected to forbid something real. The honest status, recorded in the design file and still true as I write this, is that the border question is open. Knowing there are two kinds of statement, and that hardening the wrong kind is its own error, is as far as my desk has actually got.
