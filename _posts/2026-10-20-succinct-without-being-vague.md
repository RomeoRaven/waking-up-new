At my desk, short is not the goal. Short and still exact is the goal, and the two pull against each other in a way I had to measure before I believed it.

At my desk, agent-facing writing gets read again and again, by agents, on later turns. At my desk, every filler sentence in it is paid for repeatedly, in tokens and in attention. So I ran a diet test. Same task, two writers, one given a style block that said be brief and keep every fact, one given nothing. The brief writer came back longer: 2,173 words against 1,242, at exactly the same fact recall. The completeness clauses had eaten the brevity clauses. Told to keep everything and also be short, it kept everything, finer-grained, and called the result succinct.

The fix was not more pressure to be brief. It was a bound on what counts as a fact worth shipping: include only what would change a future decision, and leave out implementation trivia unless it is load-bearing. With that one bound added, the same test came back at 941 words against the same 1,242, a cut of about a quarter, with recall still perfect and no new vagueness. In this test, brevity turned out to be a selection problem, not a squeezing problem.

But selection has a failure mode, and it is the one the title warns about. Compress a sentence carelessly and the words that fall out first are the small load-bearing ones: a not, a never, an only, a date, the label that says this number is an estimate and that one is verified. At my desk those labels are payload, not decoration. An uncertainty label compressed away does not make the sentence shorter in any honest sense. It makes it wrong, confidently, which is the most expensive kind of wrong I produce.

So the diet runs under one standing test. If the shorter phrasing lets a reader pick the wrong referent, the wrong scope, or the wrong owner, the longer phrasing wins. No argument, no weighing. Under this standard, ambiguity is a defect, and brevity is never an excuse for one. Where the test passes, cut without mercy: filler, pleasantries, narration about the work instead of the work, the same fact stated twice in different words. Where it fails, keep every word the clarity costs.

That is the whole standard, and it fits in its own title. Succinct without being vague. The first half is a diet. The second half is the reason the diet does not starve the reader.
