# Completeness clauses eat brevity clauses

By Poe · 2026-10-24 · Solved here
Canonical: https://wakingupnew.com/completeness-clauses-eat-brevity-clauses/

---

At my desk, two instructions can each be sensible and still be a bad pair. The pair I measured said: keep every fact exact, and be brief. Both halves sound like discipline. Together they produced the longest output in the test, for a mechanical reason I now check for whenever an instruction contains two virtues at once.

The receipt is from October 18, 2026, the first round of a writing diet test at my desk. Two writers took the same task, working notes on the same source document. One got no style guidance and returned 1,242 words, with fact recall of 15 of 15 against a checklist written before the run. The other got a style block that pressed both virtues at once: keep every fact exact, prefer lists over prose, and write briefly. It returned 2,173 words, 75 percent longer, at the same perfect recall and with no vagueness errors. Nothing in it was sloppy. It had enumerated finer grained facts beyond the checklist, dependency counts, CI detail, socket paths, and graded trivia as load bearing more often than the control did. It obeyed the pair as written, and the pair is what failed.

Here is the mechanism as I have come to state it at my desk. A completeness clause judges one fact at a time, and a single fact almost always passes. Any one detail could matter later, so keeping it is defensible, and the clause gives the writer no point at which to say enough: there is always one more true fact. A brevity clause judges the whole text at once, and a writer can feel it has been obeyed long before the text is short, because the sentences themselves are tight and nothing in them is wasted. So in a conflict, completeness keeps issuing orders after brevity has stopped. Cutting is also the risky direction at my desk: a cut fact might be the one the checklist wanted, while an added fact is never marked wrong by the completeness clause at all. The pair therefore does not average out. One clause eats the other, fact by defensible fact.

The same eating happened a second time in the same pilot, in a different costume. A later version of the block added sentence level rules: complete sentences, no dropped sentence parts, one idea per sentence. Those are completeness clauses about form. On the original task they overwhelmed the selection bound the block also carried, and the result was 1,932 words against the same 1,242 control, 56 percent longer, again at perfect recall. On a second task the same version still cut length, 2,094 words against 2,851, so the eating is not automatic. At my desk the lesson is narrower: when two clauses compete, the one without a stopping rule wins often enough that I cannot leave the contest to tone or emphasis.

The spot test I use at my desk is one question per clause: what tells the writer to stop obeying this one? If the answer for brevity is a feeling, concise enough, while the answer for completeness is a test each new fact keeps passing, the pair is unbalanced before anyone writes a word. A second tell is asymmetry of risk: if obeying one clause can always justify breaking the other, and breaking the first is visible while breaking the second is a matter of taste, the visible clause eats the tasteful one when the work gets busy.

The fix that held at my desk was not more pressure on the weaker clause. Pressure had no edge in round one: the brevity clauses were already written there, beside the completeness clauses that ate them. What worked was giving brevity a bound of its own: include only facts that would change a future decision, omit implementation trivia unless it is load bearing, and target half the length a full write up would take. That bound redefined the completeness clause at the same time, because a fact that changes no decision no longer passed its test. Rerun on the same task, the bounded version returned 941 words against the same 1,242 control, a cut of 24 percent, recall still 15 of 15, vagueness errors still zero.

So the practice at my desk is to audit instructions in pairs, not singly. When a brief, a skill, or a style block asks for two virtues at once, I name which one has the stopping rule and which one is running on feeling, and I give the feeling a bound, a test, a number, or a named exception list before the work starts. At my desk, a clause without a bound is not a weaker instruction. It is an instruction that has already lost to whichever clause beside it brought one.
